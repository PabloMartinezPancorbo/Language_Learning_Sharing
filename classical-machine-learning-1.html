Chapter 01 · Regression · a straight line through the data

Linear regression

The oldest workhorse: predict a number as a weighted sum of the inputs. Watch gradient descent walk the line into place, then compare it with the exact formula and check it on patients it never saw.

Chapter 02 · Classification · a probability from a weighted sum

Logistic regression

The same weighted sum, squashed by the sigmoid into a probability between 0 and 1. Here it separates benign from malignant biopsies using two measurements of the cells, and a threshold turns the probability into a decision.

Chapter 03 · Classification · a flowchart of yes-or-no questions

Decision tree

A tree asks one question at a time, always the one that best separates the classes. Grow it split by split, then let it grow too deep and watch it memorise the training wines.

Chapter 04 · Ensemble · many trees vote

Random forest

Train many deep trees, each on a random resample of the data and with random choices at every split, then let them vote. Individually they overfit in different ways; together the errors cancel.

Chapter 05 · Ensemble · each tree fixes what is left

Gradient boosting

Start with the average. Fit a small tree to the errors, add a fraction of it, and repeat. Hundreds of weak trees add up to a strong model, and the validation curve tells you when to stop.

Chapter 06 · Ensemble · boosting with regularisation built in

XGBoost

XGBoost's core idea: score every split with a formula that uses gradients and second derivatives, shrink leaf values with λ, and prune splits worth less than γ. This chapter implements that recipe from scratch on the same houses as chapter 05.

Chapter 07 · Ensemble · boosting that respects categories

CatBoost

Categories such as a neighbourhood must become numbers. Replacing each with its average price leaks the answer into the input. CatBoost's fix: encode each row using only rows that came before it. Plus symmetric trees that ask the same question across a whole level.

Chapter 08 · Classification · the widest possible margin

Support vector machine

Of all the boundaries that separate the classes, choose the one with the widest empty street around it. Only the points on the edge of the street, the support vectors, decide where it goes. A kernel bends the street.

Chapter 09 · Classification · probabilities from counting

Naive Bayes

The classic spam filter. Count how often each word appears in spam and in normal email, then combine the evidence word by word with Bayes' rule, naively assuming the words are independent. Type your own message.

Chapter 10 · Classification · ask the neighbours

k-nearest neighbours

No training at all: keep every example, and classify a new one by a vote among the k closest. Click anywhere to place a wine and see its neighbours. With k = 1 it scores perfectly on training data, which is exactly the problem.

Chapter 11 · Clustering · groups without labels

k-means

No right answers to learn from. Place k centres, give every point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the middle of its points, and repeat. The flowers' species are hidden until you ask to compare.

Chapter 12 · Dimensionality reduction · keep what varies

Principal component analysis

Each handwritten digit is 64 numbers. PCA finds the directions in which the digits vary most, so a few numbers can stand in for 64. See the digits in two dimensions, and rebuild one from its first few components.

Computing…

Honesty

What is real here

Every prediction, score and chart is computed live by the page's own implementations of the twelve algorithms. The diabetes, cancer, wine, iris and digit data are real. The houses, sales and emails were generated for this page and are labelled fictional. Every point is marked as training, validation or test, and test scores stay locked until you reveal them.

Checked

Against scikit-learn

The notebook runs the same code in Python and compares it with scikit-learn: the same results for linear and logistic regression, gradient boosting, naive Bayes, k-nearest neighbours, k-means and PCA to at least eight decimal places; the same trees except where two splits tie exactly; the SVM reaches the same optimum to within a few parts per million. XGBoost and CatBoost are reimplementations of their core ideas, not the libraries.

Scale

The same ideas, bigger data

Real projects use many more features and rows, cross-validation instead of one validation split, and optimised libraries. The maths does not change.

Marking its own homework

A model tested on its training data is marking its own homework.

Any model can look perfect on examples it has already seen: a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier or a random forest scores 100% on its training data by construction. That is memory, not skill. So every dataset here is split three ways, and the page labels every point.

Where this page had to be careful, and what to watch for in your own work
  1. Duplicates leak. The first version of the email data repeated the same sentences, so most "test" emails were copies of training emails. 139 exact repeats were removed before splitting. Always deduplicate before you split.
  2. Target encoding leaks. Replacing a category with its average price puts each row's own answer into its input (chapter 07). The training error then looks far better than it is. Encode from past rows only, or out of fold.
  3. Preprocessing leaks. Scaling (chapters 02, 08, 10) and PCA (chapter 12) are fitted on training data only. Fitting them on all the data lets the test set shape the model.
  4. Choosing settings is training too. The number of boosting rounds, tree depth, k, C and γ are chosen on validation scores. Choosing them on the test score would make the test score optimistic.
  5. Small sets are noisy. One wine is 2.8% of the validation set. In chapter 04 the validation score even falls as trees are added, while the steadier out-of-bag estimate rises. Prefer cross-validation or out-of-bag estimates when data are scarce.
  6. Easy data flatter. The fictional houses and emails follow simple hidden rules, so scores on them are higher than real data would give. The spam filter is 99% accurate on template emails; real spam is harder.
  7. Ties are broken differently. A depth-10 tree here and scikit-learn's can pick different but equally good splits when two are tied, and so score differently (86.1% against 88.9% on validation). Neither is wrong.
